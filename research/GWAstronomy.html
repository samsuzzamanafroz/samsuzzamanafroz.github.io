What the black holes are telling us

Every binary black hole that LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA detect carries a record of how it was born: the masses of its two black holes, how fast they spin, and how long the pair took to merge after its stars formed. One event says little on its own, but a catalogue of hundreds of them starts to reveal the population behind them, and with it the lives and deaths of massive stars across cosmic time.

This line of my research reads that record, one property at a time. Each chapter below asks a separate question about the population, using the public LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA catalogues and methods that correct for which black holes the detectors can and cannot see.

Chapter 1 · Black hole mass

Is the black hole mass function changing over cosmic time?

The masses of binary black holes are shaped by the stars that made them. How much mass a massive star loses to winds before it collapses depends strongly on its metallicity, the fraction of elements heavier than helium. In the early Universe, stars were more metal-poor, lost less mass and could leave behind heavier black holes. If that is what happens, the distribution of black hole masses, the mass function, should change with redshift: heavy black holes should become relatively more common the further back in time we look.

This leads to two open questions. Does a single mass distribution describe binary black holes at all redshifts, or only within a limited range? And if the distribution does evolve, how and where in mass does it change? Answering them is difficult, because we cannot predict the mass function from first principles and because the detectors see heavy, nearby binaries much more easily than light, distant ones.

1.1 — The methodLetting the data describe the evolution

Most earlier studies assumed a specific shape for the mass function, such as a power law with a peak, and let only a few parameters change with redshift. That can hide features nobody thought to model. We took a non-parametric approach instead: we describe the joint distribution of mass and redshift as a smooth expansion around a reference redshift of z = 0.25, keeping terms up to second order.

p(m,z) \approx p_0(m) + p_1(m)\,(z-z_{\rm ref}) + p_2(m)\,(z-z_{\rm ref})^2

Each term has a direct meaning, and each is measured separately in every mass bin, so the data decide where in mass any evolution happens:

p0(m): the mass function today

The shape of the black hole mass distribution at the reference redshift, the baseline against which any change is measured.

p1(m): linear evolution

Whether black holes of a given mass were more common (positive) or less common (negative) at higher redshift. Its size tells us how strong the evolution is.

p2(m): curvature

Whether that change speeds up or slows down with redshift. This term allows non-monotonic behaviour, where a trend strengthens or saturates.

Selection effects, handled

Detection probability is measured from the public LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA injection campaigns and built into a hierarchical Bayesian likelihood, together with the full measurement uncertainty of every event.

1.2 — The dataBinary black holes in the mass–redshift plane

We used the publicly released parameter-estimation samples of the binary black hole mergers in the GWTC-3 and GWTC-4 catalogues. For every event, each posterior sample of the source-frame component masses and luminosity distance was converted to redshift (assuming Planck 2018 cosmology), so each event becomes a cloud of possible positions in the mass–redshift plane. That is exactly why the correction for detector sensitivity matters. An apparent trend between mass and distance can come from two sources that are not exclusive: a pure selection bias, because heavy binaries are visible further out, or a genuine change in the population, for example driven by metallicity. Only after correcting for what the detectors can see can the two be told apart.

1.3 — ResultsHeavier black holes show a mild trend with redshift

We ran the analysis in the mass–redshift plane for masses of 10–80 solar masses and redshifts up to 1, with two different bin sizes to check that the result does not depend on how the data are divided. The pattern is the same each time. Below about 30 solar masses the linear term is consistent with zero: light black holes show no measurable evolution. Above roughly 40 solar masses it moves systematically to positive values, a mild hint that massive black holes were relatively more common at earlier cosmic times. The quadratic term stays consistent with zero everywhere, so a simple linear trend is enough up to redshift about 1.

These results fit between earlier findings from GWTC-3 alone, which ranged from hints that the characteristic mass increases with redshift to results consistent with no evolution at all. Adding GWTC-4, with its larger sample and greater redshift reach, lets a mass-resolved analysis show where in mass any evolution is concentrated: among the heavy black holes, not the light ones.

1.4 — What it meansA fingerprint of metallicity

The mass-dependent pattern matches what metallicity-driven evolution predicts. At high redshift, metal-poor stars have weaker winds, keep more of their mass and can leave heavier black holes, so the massive end of the mass function grows with redshift. Lighter black holes, which form through more robust core-collapse channels, are less sensitive to their environment and show no measurable change. The gradual, linear form of the trend points to a slow change in the chemical make-up of the Universe rather than an abrupt transition. It also agrees with my phase-space analyses, which indicate a distinct population of binary black holes above about 45 solar masses.

The evidence is still mild, limited by the number of heavy, distant events. Next-generation detectors such as Cosmic Explorer and the Einstein Telescope will detect binary black holes out to much higher redshift, and the same non-parametric method will then be able to measure higher-order features of the evolution precisely, linking gravitational waves to stellar evolution and the chemical history of galaxies.

Chapter 2 · Delay times · Flagship project

How long do black hole binaries take to merge?

A binary black hole does not merge the moment its stars are born. First the two massive stars must live out their lives and collapse; then the black holes must spiral together, slowly losing orbital energy to gravitational waves. The time between the birth of the stars and the final merger is the delay time. It can be a few hundred million years for a tight pair or longer than the age of the Universe for a wide one, and how these delays are distributed records how the binaries were made: how close the stars were at birth, how they exchanged mass and shed their envelopes, and whether they formed in isolation or were assembled in dense star clusters.

No single event reveals its delay. But the delays shape the whole population: the merger rate we observe at each redshift is the cosmic star formation history, smeared forward in time by the delay time distribution (DTD):

\mathcal{R}(z) \;\propto\; \int_z^{\infty} p\big(t(z)-t(z_f)\big)\;\mathrm{SFR}(z_f)\;\Big|\frac{dt}{dz_f}\Big|\,dz_f

Short delays make the merger rate follow star formation closely; long delays shift mergers towards today. Measuring how the binary black hole merger rate changes with redshift therefore tells us how long the binaries took to merge, and this project reconstructs that distribution directly from the latest LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA catalogue.

The question: what does the delay time distribution of binary black holes look like when we let the data decide its shape, and is it the same for light and heavy black holes?

2.1 — Beyond a power lawWhy not just fit a power law?

Almost every earlier constraint assumed that delay times follow a power law, p(t_d)\propto t_d^{\alpha} above some minimum delay, and measured its slope. With GWTC-3 these studies found no evidence for anything more complex. But a power law is a strong assumption: it has the same logarithmic slope everywhere, so it cannot describe a distribution that stays flat and then falls sharply, or one that rises to a peak and declines. Binary evolution can produce such shapes, for example when several formation pathways or mass-dependent physics contribute, and a fixed formula would hide them. The aim here was to measure the shape itself.

2.2 — The methodTesting every shape on a grid

Instead of choosing a formula, we lay a grid over the plane of delay time and probability and evaluate every distribution that can be drawn through it.

1Enumerate

Every candidate shape

Seven delay-time nodes, spaced logarithmically from 0.5 to 12 billion years (0.50, 0.85, 1.44, 2.45, 4.16, 7.07, 12.0 Gyr), each take one of five probability levels spanning a factor of 100. Every combination, smoothly interpolated with a monotone spline and normalized, is one candidate DTD, a "trajectory": 57 = 78,125 in all.

3Rank

Bayesian evidence

A hierarchical Poisson likelihood compares the prediction with the observed events, including their full measurement uncertainty, and gives every trajectory a Bayesian evidence and a local merger rate. The best-supported trajectories form the reconstruction.

Nothing assumed, everything tested

No smoothness is imposed between nodes, so the grid contains plateaus, peaks, dips and sharp cut-offs. Power laws are among its trajectories, so they can be compared on exactly the same footing.

A fixed mass model

The masses follow the fiducial "Broken Power Law + 2 Peaks" model of the GWTC-5.0 population analysis, with its parameters held fixed, so that the redshift information goes entirely into the delay times.

Selection effects included

The detection probability, for a false-alarm rate below one per year, comes from the LVK injection campaign and enters the likelihood directly, so heavier events seen to larger distances do not bias the result.

Which shapes count

The reconstruction shows all trajectories within a Bayes factor of 3 of the best one, and summarizes them by evidence-weighted medians and 90% intervals.

2.3 — The dataGWTC-5.0, in two mass bins

We used the cumulative GWTC-5.0 catalogue, which includes all events from GWTC-2.1, GWTC-3 and GWTC-4.0 together with the second part of the fourth observing run, keeping the binary black holes detected with a false-alarm rate below one per year in at least one search. To ask whether the delays depend on mass, we split the population by the mass of the heavier black hole into 20–40 M☉ and 40–100 M☉, a boundary chosen so that both bins have similar statistical power, and reconstructed the DTD of each bin independently. Each event contributes to a bin in proportion to how much of its posterior falls inside it.

2.4 — Star formationThree histories of star formation

The delay times are only half of the convolution; the other half is when the progenitor stars formed. Not all star formation makes binary black holes equally well: metal-rich stars lose much of their mass to winds, so massive black holes form mostly from metal-poor stars, which were more common in the early Universe.

We therefore repeated the full analysis for three star formation histories: the standard, metallicity-averaged Madau–Dickinson model, and two observation-based histories that keep only star formation above or below one-tenth of the solar metallicity. The high-metallicity history closely follows Madau–Dickinson; the low-metallicity one rises far more steeply towards the early Universe. Comparing the three shows how much the inferred delays depend on where the stars were born.

2.5 — ResultsA plateau, then a steep fall after a few billion years

In both mass bins and for all three star formation histories, the data favour the same overall shape: an extended plateau of delay times up to about 2–3 billion years, a maximum at intermediate delays, and a steep decline towards the longest delays. For the Madau–Dickinson and high-metallicity histories, the median DTD peaks at about 2.4 Gyr, is above half its maximum from about 1.3 to 3.1 Gyr, and falls by a factor of about 80 between the maximum and 7 Gyr. With the low-metallicity history, the support concentrates more sharply, with the maximum at about 4.1 Gyr.

Convolving these delays with star formation gives the merger rate across cosmic time. In every case it rises from its local value to a peak near redshift 1, below the peak of star formation at redshift ~1.8, exactly as delays of a few billion years imply, and then falls. For the Madau–Dickinson history the lighter bin rises from about 24 to a peak of about 114 mergers per cubic gigaparsec per year at redshift 1; the heavier bin starts from about 13 and peaks near 66 at redshift 1.1. Across all three histories the best-fit local rates are 19–25 and 9–13 Gpc⁻³ yr⁻¹ for the lighter and heavier bin. The low-metallicity history produces by far the sharpest peak, about 11–12 times the local rate.

The Bayesian evidence of all 78,125 trajectories, plotted against the local rate each one predicts, shows how strongly the data discriminate between shapes. The best trajectories sit at the lowest local rates, and the evidence falls by roughly 180–250 units of ln Z towards the worst ones, which over-predict the observed events. The striped families come from the five probability levels at the longest-delay node, with which the local rate is strongly correlated.

The heavier bin has fewer trajectories within a Bayes factor of 3 and a somewhat narrower interval, because its events are seen out to larger redshift and sample more of the merger-rate evolution.

Since the two bins share some events, we also compared one DTD shared by both bins with two independent ones. A shared DTD is preferred for every star formation history and bin boundary: the data do not yet require different delay times for light and heavy black holes. This does not rule out a mass dependence, which population-synthesis models predict; it means that today's catalogue cannot yet detect one.

2.6 — Power lawsThe data prefer a shape a power law cannot draw

Because power laws are themselves trajectories of the grid, the two can be compared directly. Scanning the minimum delay over the grid nodes and the slope from −6 to 0, the best grid trajectory beats the best power law in every case, by Δln Z = 1.3 to 4.7 depending on the mass bin and star formation history. The best power laws need minimum delays of 1.4–4.2 Gyr and very steep slopes, α ≈ −3.3 to −6, and even then cannot reproduce a flat plateau followed by a sharp decline, or a rise to a peak followed by a fall. The preferred delay time distribution is not a power law.

2.7 — RobustnessStress-testing the result

A flexible method needs careful checks. We repeated the analysis while changing every choice that could plausibly drive the result.

A coarser grid

Six delay nodes instead of seven over the same range reproduce every qualitative conclusion: the plateau, the decline, the merger-rate peak and the lower local rate of heavy black holes.

Shorter minimum delays

Starting the grid at 100 or 200 Myr instead of 0.5 Gyr changes the evidence by about one unit or less and the best local rate by under 2% for the Madau–Dickinson and high-metallicity histories. The low-metallicity result is less robust to this choice.

Lighter black holes too

Extending the low-mass bin down to 5 M☉ gives the same shape: a single maximum at intermediate delays and a decline by a factor of ~70–90 towards 7 Gyr, so the result holds for the bulk of the population.

Where the bins are split

Moving the boundary between the bins to 35 or 45 M☉ moves the DTD maximum by at most one grid node; the best-fit DTDs agree to within about one node.

The metallicity threshold

Changing the threshold from 0.1 to 0.05 or 0.2 Z☉ leaves the results essentially unchanged; local rates move by at most 3%.

A smooth formation efficiency

Replacing the hard metallicity cut with an efficiency that declines smoothly with metallicity reproduces the low-metallicity result.

The star formation history and the delay times enter only through their convolution, and they are strongly degenerate over the redshifts current detectors reach. The low-metallicity history gives the highest evidence, but this should not be read as a measurement of how black hole formation depends on metallicity: for any assumed history, the delays adjust so that the predicted merger rate matches the data.

2.8 — What it meansDelays of a few billion years

Letting the data choose the shape, the binary black holes seen so far are best described by delays that are typically a few billion years, with a plateau up to ~2–3 Gyr and a steep cut-off beyond ~4 Gyr, a shape no single power law can capture. The merger rate rises to a peak near redshift 1, below the peak of star formation, and light and heavy black holes are so far consistent with sharing the same delays. Just as importantly, the grid approach shows clearly which parts of the distribution are measured and which are not.

The main limitation today is the short-delay end of the distribution, and with it the local merger rate: to measure delays below a billion years, detectors must see beyond the peak of cosmic star formation. Future observing runs and, above all, next-generation detectors such as Cosmic Explorer and the Einstein Telescope will do exactly that, sharpening the short delays and testing whether black holes of different masses merge on different timescales, a key prediction of binary evolution models.
